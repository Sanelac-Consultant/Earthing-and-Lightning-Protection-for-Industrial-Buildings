## Soil Data Comes Before the Drawing

Every solid earthing design starts in the ground, not on the CAD screen. Soil resistivity changes with moisture, temperature, compaction, and salt content, and the alluvial, sandy-silt profile across much of Delhi NCR can swing sharply between a dry May and a wet August. A single reading taken on one convenient afternoon is a snapshot, not a design basis. A dependable survey uses the Wenner four-pin method at several spacings, notes the weather of the preceding weeks, and applies a seasonal correction so the grid is sized for the worst month rather than the best one. The guidance in IS 3043 supports exactly this approach. When the resistivity report is thin, electrodes end up oversized in the wrong places and undersized where the fault current really flows. Good site data costs a day of fieldwork and saves years of nuisance tripping.

## Building an Earthing Grid That Serves Every Load

Industrial sites rarely have one kind of load. A press shop, a bank of induction furnaces, an effluent treatment plant, a control room full of PLCs, and a rooftop solar array can all sit on the same campus, and each reacts differently to a fault. That is why an [**Electrical Design Consultant Delhi**](https://sanelac.com/service/electrical-design-services/) factories brought in early will usually propose a bonded mesh or ring grid laid around the building footprint, tied to structural steel and column rebar, with copper-bonded or galvanised rods driven down to permanently moist strata.

The older habit of keeping separate “clean” and “dirty” earths has largely given way to a single, well-bonded system, because two isolated earths can develop a dangerous potential difference during a surge. Conductor sizing follows the prospective fault current and its clearing time, not the budget line. Chemical or maintenance-free electrodes earn their place in rocky or dry pockets, but they are a tool for specific soil conditions, not a default for every pit. Inspection chambers, labelled test links and clean as-built drawings make future maintenance far easier.

## Lightning Protection Starts With a Risk Number

Fitting air terminals on a roof and calling it protection is the quickest route to a false sense of safety. A proper design begins with a risk assessment in line with IS/IEC 62305, weighing the building’s size, height, location, contents and the consequences of failure. The result sets the protection level and, with it, the geometry of the system: rolling sphere coverage for air terminals, down conductor spacing, and bonding rules for everything that touches the roof.

Where structural steel is electrically continuous, it can carry much of the current, but only if the joints are verified rather than assumed. Extra care is needed around flammable stores, dust-prone processes and tall stacks, where a strike means a shutdown, an insurance claim or worse. Early-streamer devices are still sold aggressively, yet a conservative designer sticks to the methods the standard actually recognises.

Three details get skipped more often than any others:

- Test joints on every down conductor, so continuity can be verified without opening up the structure.
- Bonding of roof-mounted equipment such as solar arrays, HVAC units and cable trays to the lightning system.
- Adequate separation distance between the external system and internal cabling or metal services.

## Surge Protection, the Layer Most Plants Underspend On

Lightning does not have to hit the building to damage it. A strike hundreds of metres away can push a surge along incoming cables and into drives, controllers and networking gear that cost far more than the copper in the grid. Staged surge protection handles this: Type 1 devices at the main incomer, Type 2 at distribution boards, and Type 3 close to sensitive equipment.

Short, straight connection leads matter as much as the device rating, because every extra metre of wire adds inductance and lets more of the surge through. ## Testing, Records and 2026 Expectations

A system is only as good as its last measurement. Earth resistance should be tested at commissioning and then at least annually, with a check before and after the monsoon to catch seasonal drift. Bond continuity, joint condition, and surge device status indicators deserve the same attention. Keep results in a logbook or digital register so trends become visible, because a slow rise in resistance is a warning long before it becomes a failure.

The 2026 picture adds new pressure. Rooftop solar, EV charging bays, battery storage and dense automation are turning plain sheds into electronically sensitive sites, and insurers and auditors are asking sharper questions. Continuous earth monitoring and remote surge counters are moving from luxury to sensible practice on larger campuses.

## FAQs

**Q1. Is one earth pit enough for an industrial building?**

No. A single pit almost never satisfies fault-current and resistance targets for a factory. Industrial sites typically need multiple electrodes connected into a ring or mesh, sized from soil resistivity and fault level. Redundancy also keeps protection intact when one electrode dries out or corrodes.

**Q2. Should lightning and power earthing be connected together?**

Yes, they should be bonded into one common system. Separate electrodes can develop dangerous voltage differences during a strike, stressing insulation and sparking between metalwork. Bonding creates an equipotential plane, keeping touch voltages low for staff and protecting connected electronics, while the grid still disperses lightning current into the soil safely.

**Q3. Which earth resistance value suits a factory earthing grid?**

Most industrial designs at larger sites target one ohm or lower for the main grid, though the exact figure depends on fault level and touch-voltage limits. Lightning electrodes are judged on layout and impedance more than a single number. The design report should state the target, the calculation, and the tested result.

**Q4. Is periodic testing of lightning protection mandatory?**

Yes, periodic inspection is expected under IS/IEC 62305 and is practically required by most insurers and safety audits. Visual checks and continuity tests are usually carried out annually, with earth resistance measured before and after the monsoon. Test records protect the owner if a claim or inspection follows.
